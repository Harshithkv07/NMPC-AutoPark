REVERSING AN ARTICULATED TRACTOR-TRAILER INTO A PARKING BAY:
A JACKKNIFE-SAFE GUIDANCE SYSTEM USING HYBRID A* AND NONLINEAR MPC

Harshith K.V, G.Venugopalan, Rithvik Arulprakash, Vipin Sudhakar
School of Artificial Intelligence
Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham, Coimbatore


================================================================================
ABSTRACT
================================================================================

Learner drivers spend weeks on reverse-trailer parking before they get it
right, and the reason is less about hand-eye coordination than most people
think. The hitch dynamics are open-loop unstable in reverse. Push the rig
backward and the eigenvalue of the hitch-angle equation goes positive;
any little bend grows instead of settling. We built a complete guidance
system that handles the reversing case on an ordinary laptop. A Hybrid
A* planner produces a feasible route, using motion primitives we
stabilise in closed form against the reversing instability. A nonlinear
model-predictive controller (NMPC) then tracks the plan, with a hard
60-degree cap on the hitch angle. We ran it on 110 bays across cross,
angled, parallel and tandem layouts, across six randomised fills. Every
bay certifies on the first attempt. Peak hitch angle: 50.1 degrees.
Median parked error ran between 0.11 m and 0.23 m, depending on layout.
A plain geometric baseline, driving the same bays with no awareness of
the hitch constraint, folds the rig on all 60 reverse-in bays. Per-bay
solving time sits around 5 s, with the NMPC closing each 250 ms control
step in about 12 ms.

Index Terms: nonholonomic systems, articulated vehicles, model
predictive control, Hybrid A*, tractor-trailer, parking, jackknife
avoidance.


================================================================================
I. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

Ask any truck driver what the hardest part of the test was.
Reverse-parking a loaded trailer into a bay almost always comes up. It
is not about guessing where the wheels are. The hitch itself fights
back.

Picture it. A tractor pinned to a trailer with one hinge. Rolled
forward, the trailer trails behind politely, no drama. Rolled backward,
something goes wrong. One degree of bend turns into ten. Ten into
thirty. By the time you register it, the thing has folded on you.

Mathematically the setup is a planar two-link chain. A tractor with a
steered front axle tows a box trailer by a single pin joint, the hitch.
Four scalars capture the kinematics: tractor rear-axle position
(x1, y1), tractor heading theta_0, and hitch angle psi = theta_0 -
theta_1. The driver has exactly two levers, speed v and steering angle
delta. The hitch is passive. Whatever the no-slip constraint at the
trailer axle forces it to do, that is what it does.

A short algebra pass (Section III walks through it) gives the linearised
rate of change of psi as roughly -v/L_2 times psi, plus a term in the
steering input. Watch the sign of that coefficient. It flips with v.
Forward motion damps psi down toward zero. Reverse motion blows it up.
At 1 m/s on our 8 m trailer wheelbase the doubling time is around
5.5 s. A one-degree starting error turns into a jackknife in about half
a minute, assuming no one does anything about it.

Which is where this project sits.

Section IV covers the Hybrid A* planner, whose primitives come from
closed-form inversion of psi-dot and so stay inside the stable region
in both gears. Section V covers the NMPC that tracks the planned path
with the 60-degree hitch limit as a hard inequality at every step of a
9.6 s prediction horizon. Section VI is the second-attempt ladder,
which lets the solver recover a bay the way a human driver would: pull
forward, re-plan, try again. Section VII, implementation. Section VIII,
results against the 110-bay library and two baselines. Limitations
last.

What we actually added:

- A stabilised set of Hybrid A* primitives that respect the hitch-angle
  dynamics in both gears, derived from the closed-form inversion of
  psi-dot.
- A fast NMPC implementation that batches the finite-difference
  Jacobian through a single numpy evaluation of RK4 and its eight
  forward-difference neighbours, closing a control step in about
  12 ms.
- A certified end-to-end benchmark on 110 bays, with a regression gate
  we actually trust, and a geometric baseline that is not a strawman.

Nothing exotic underneath. CPython, numpy, scipy. That is the whole
scientific stack.


================================================================================
II. RELATED WORK
================================================================================

Three papers frame what we built.

Alenchery et al. [1] is the closest match to our problem. The preprint
proposes the exact pipeline we settled on, sensor fusion feeding
Hybrid A*, then NMPC for the trailer, with LQR for low-level actuation.
In the paper itself only the Hybrid A* and a B-spline smoother are
actually implemented; the NMPC and LQR stages are described but never
coded, and the reported closed-loop runs jackknife on the harder reverse
bays. We take that as our starting point: build the NMPC stage the
preprint omits, impose the hitch-angle limit the preprint lacks, and
run the whole thing closed-loop.

Beglini et al. [2] come at the hitch-angle problem from the control
side. They design an intrinsically stable MPC (IS-MPC) for a tractor-
trailer that follows a given reference path and guarantees |psi| stays
bounded, even in reverse. The paper handles no planning, no obstacles,
and no parking manoeuvre. We treat it as the anti-jackknifing invariant
we want to preserve, then push the scope outward: our NMPC enforces the
same hitch-angle constraint while tracking a Hybrid A* plan through a
yard full of parked trucks.

Kayacan et al. [3] is the agricultural reference. Their tube-based
decentralised NMPC drives an autonomous tractor pulling a trailed
implement along crop rows for field work; the reported cross-track
accuracy in straight sections is 5-8 cm. The paper establishes what
tractor-trailer MPC accuracy is reasonable to expect in open terrain,
which is the benchmark we use to interpret our own 7.95 cm and 5.42 cm
straight-line errors in the parking context.

The three papers partition the space neatly. Alenchery brings the
pipeline but leaves the controller unbuilt. Beglini brings the
hitch-stable controller but leaves out planning and the yard. Kayacan
brings the field accuracy benchmark but not the on-road parking task.
Our contribution sits at the intersection: Alenchery's pipeline, with
Beglini's hitch guarantee, benchmarked against Kayacan's accuracy
regime, applied to on-road reverse parking into a tight yard.


================================================================================
III. SYSTEM MODEL
================================================================================

A. State and inputs

Let (x1, y1) be the position of the tractor rear axle in the world
frame. Let theta_0 be the tractor heading. Let psi = theta_0 - theta_1
be the hitch angle, with theta_1 the trailer heading. The state is

    X = (x1, y1, theta_0, psi).

Two inputs: signed tractor speed v (positive forward), and front-wheel
steering angle delta. Rig parameters: tractor wheelbase L1, trailer
wheelbase (hitch to trailer axle) L2, hitch offset d (ahead of the
tractor rear axle, along the tractor's longitudinal axis). Our defaults:
L1 = 4 m, L2 = 8 m, d = 0.5 m, |delta| <= 35 deg, |v| <= 2 m/s.

B. The four-state kinematic ODE

Under the no-slip assumption, which holds to a few centimetres below
2.5 m/s on dry asphalt,

    x1_dot      = v cos(theta_0)
    y1_dot      = v sin(theta_0)
    theta_0_dot = (v / L1) tan(delta)
    psi_dot     = v [ tan(delta)/L1
                     - sin(psi)/L2
                     - d tan(delta) cos(psi) / (L1 L2) ].

The first three are the standard rear-axle bicycle model. The fourth
drops out of applying the trailer-axle no-slip constraint to the
forward kinematics of the hitch and then using psi_dot = theta_0_dot -
theta_1_dot. The full six-step derivation lives in our kinematics
reference (doc/tractor-trailer-kinematics.md); we would reproduce it
here except we would run out of column space.

Three physical terms sit inside those brackets. First, tan(delta)/L1 is
the rate at which steering alone would spin the tractor if the trailer
did not move. Second, -sin(psi)/L2 is the no-slip reaction from the
trailer axle. In forward motion this term is the stabiliser; in reverse
it is the destabiliser. Third, the hitch is not exactly over the rear
axle, so there is a small correction term. Our rig has d = 0.5 m, which
makes this contribution a few per cent at most.

C. Why reversing is unstable

Linearise around psi = 0, delta = 0:

    psi_dot ~ A psi + B(delta, v),   A = -v/L2,
    B = (v delta / L1) (1 - d/L2).

Forward motion: A is negative, so the hitch self-aligns with time
constant L2/v. Reverse motion: A turns positive, so the hitch angle
doubles every L2 ln(2)/|v| seconds. Plug in our numbers at 1 m/s and
the doubling time is roughly 5.5 s.

Everything in our architecture gets built around that one scalar. A
controller that tracks psi as another output with the usual tracking
cost inherits the instability of the plant. A controller that treats
psi as a hard state constraint, as our NMPC does, holds the rig inside
the feasible region whether the dynamics want to drift or not.

D. Flat output

The trailer axle position is a differentially flat output of the
system. Any smooth trailer-axle trajectory (x_T(t), y_T(t)) with
bounded curvature determines the full state and both inputs in closed
form, with psi = arctan(L2 * kappa).

We lean on this twice. First in the planner: the heuristic between any
two nodes is a Dubins path on the trailer axle, scaled by the
admissible curvature kappa_max = tan(psi_crit)/L2. Second in the NMPC
cost: the reference is defined on the trailer axle rather than the
tractor rear axle, which gives a smoother commanded-steering profile
near the goal and avoids oscillation on the final approach into the
bay.


================================================================================
IV. HYBRID A* WITH STABILISED PRIMITIVES
================================================================================

Grid A* cannot plan a tractor-trailer manoeuvre out of the box. Grid
cells do not respect the nonholonomic constraints, and a straight line
between two neighbouring cells is just not drivable. Hybrid A* bridges
that gap by expanding each node not through its grid neighbours, but by
simulating a batch of short motion primitives, keeping the continuous
poses that result, and snapping to the grid only for duplicate
detection.

A. Primitives tuned to the hitch dynamics

Our lattice state is (x, y, theta_0, psi), at a grid resolution of
0.25 m in position, 10 deg in theta_0, and 5 deg in psi. A node
expansion runs ten primitives: five target hitch angles psi_des in
{-45, -25, 0, +25, +45} degrees, each in both gears. Each primitive
runs for 0.6 s with a time step of 0.1 s, so it corresponds to six RK4
sub-steps of the ODE.

The steering input is not pulled from a fixed grid. Given a target
psi_des, we solve the psi-dot equation for the delta that pushes psi
toward psi_des with a chosen first-order time constant. Substituting
that delta turns every primitive into a short closed-form segment of a
hitch-angle regulator. In forward motion the regulator is a mild
stabiliser, nothing more. In reverse it is carrying the whole thing.
We tested this directly: without the regulator, a plain reverse-gear
primitive wanders out of the 60-degree band inside one or two
expansions.

The ten primitives from a sample node sit in two fans, the reverse-gear
fan on the left and the forward-gear fan on the right. Every curve
plotted is the trajectory of the trailer axle, not the tractor rear
axle. The distinction matters because the trailer axle is the flat
output and it is what the collision check at the end of a reverse-in
actually cares about.

B. Heuristic and analytic shot

Two speed-up tricks worth stating for reproducibility, both standard in
Hybrid A*.

First, the heuristic between any state and the goal is a Dubins path on
the trailer axle. This is a valid lower bound on time, because any
drivable trajectory of the full rig projects to a trailer-axle curve of
at most the same length.

Second, every expansion attempts an analytic shot. A Dubins path from
the current trailer-axle pose to the goal, pushed through the inverse
kinematics, and discarded if it violates clearance or the hitch-angle
limit. On easy bays the analytic shot picks the goal up inside the
first few expansions. On harder bays the planner falls back to
primitive expansion and grinds on it for a while.

Node expansion, collision check and cost update are all vectorised. For
a batch of ten primitives we evaluate the ODE across 60 time steps in a
single numpy call, instead of ten Python loops. That alone keeps the
planner's share of the per-bay budget to roughly half of the total.


================================================================================
V. NONLINEAR MPC TRACKER
================================================================================

A. Problem statement

At each control step k (period 0.25 s) the tracker solves a nonlinear
program:

    minimise over delta_{0:N-1}:
        sum_{i=0..N}   || X_{k+i} - X_ref_{k+i} ||^2_Q
      + sum_{i=0..N-1} || delta_i ||^2_R
      + sum_{i=0..N-1} || delta_i - delta_{i-1} ||^2_S

    subject to:
        X_{k+i+1} = f(X_{k+i}, v_{k+i}, delta_i),  i = 0..N-1
        |delta_i| <= delta_max
        |psi_{k+i}| <= psi_crit = 60 deg,  i = 0..N
        sdf(X_{k+i}) >= c_min,              i = 0..N

With N = 24 and step length 0.4 s, the horizon is 9.6 s. That works out
to roughly twice the hitch-instability doubling time at 1 m/s, which is
the shortest horizon that still corrects disturbances before they can
grow into jackknife territory. Shorter horizons we tried caught four
out of five reverse bays. The fifth folded.

The decision variables are not the 24 individual steering angles. We
block the horizon into eight piecewise-constant segments of three steps
each (N_B = 8, block length N/N_B = 3), dropping the degree of freedom
from 24 to 8.

Weights (from sim/tracker.py). Stage cost per step: trailer position
0.6, trailer heading 1.5, hitch angle psi 15.0, tractor position 0.15,
tractor heading 0.3. Terminal cost at k + N: trailer position 8.0,
trailer heading 60.0, hitch angle 60.0, tractor position 1.0. Steering
penalty R = 0.02, steering-rate penalty S = 3.0. The hitch weight
dominates by design, because when the controller is forced to trade
off cost terms we prefer a straighter rig over a slightly tighter
trajectory.

X_ref is defined on the trailer axle, which is why the dominant stage
weights sit on the trailer-axle pose and on psi. The tractor-axle pose
carries lower weight because, under reverse motion, the tractor rear
axle is the non-minimum-phase output of the chain. The speed profile
v_k comes from the planner (ramp-up, cruise, ramp-down per segment);
it is not a decision variable at the control step, and respects
v_fwd <= 2.2 m/s, |v_rev| <= 1.2 m/s. Clearance uses a signed-distance
function over the yard geometry, evaluated at every covering disc of
the rig footprint. The NMPC maintains an inner margin of
c_min = 0.25 m; the final certification criterion is tighter again,
requiring 0.10 m between any corner of the rig and any obstacle.

B. Solver and warm start

We solve the NLP with scipy.optimize.minimize using the SLSQP method
and its finite-difference Jacobian estimator. SLSQP is not the fastest
NLP solver out there. It is good enough for our horizon size, it
handles the equality and inequality mix without pulling in external
dependencies, and it tends to converge in two to five iterations per
control step. Each solve is warm-started from the previous step's
optimum, shifted by one step and extrapolated at the tail.

C. Batched Jacobian

The finite-difference Jacobian is batched. A naive implementation would
simulate the horizon once per gradient evaluation, then simulate it
again with each of the eight inputs perturbed by the forward-difference
step (1.49e-8, the numpy default). Nine horizon rollouts per Jacobian
call, inside every line search. That does not fit a 250 ms control
budget on a laptop sharing cycles with the viewer.

Our batched form (model.step_batch) pushes a (9, 4) array of states
and a (9,) array of inputs through RK4 in a single call. The cost,
hitch-limit constraints, steering-rate constraint and clearance
constraint all reduce to numpy operations on the resulting
(9, N+1, 4) tensor. For a single line-search probe we fall back to the
plain-Python RK4, which is actually faster when it is just one
trajectory. Average solve time comes out to about 12 ms, well inside
the control period.

D. Clipping the horizon at the end of a segment

The prediction horizon is clipped at the end of the planned segment.
Once the predicted index runs past the final planned step, we hold the
predicted state and zero the speed. Without that clip, the predicted
rig keeps rolling at constant speed and clips any obstacle sitting
behind the target bay, so the NMPC starts swerving around a collision
that is never actually going to happen. The guard lives in tracker.py
as STOP_AT_END. One line of code, with a disproportionate amount of
correctness attached to it.


================================================================================
VI. SECOND-ATTEMPT LADDER AND CERTIFICATION
================================================================================

Real drivers who end up crooked, or too close to a neighbour, stop,
pull forward, and have another go. We encoded that behaviour in the
solver as a ladder of attempts (sim/export.py). The first attempt is
the one that goes into the library; if it certifies, the ladder never
runs. If it does not, the solver works through the ladder in order:

1. Pull forward from the stopped pose and re-plan into the bay, up to
   two more legs.
2. If a predicted collision is imminent, stop early at 0.15 m
   clearance and continue from there.
3. Re-plan from the gate with a gentler search weight and a larger
   clearance margin, each option followed by the pull-forward
   recovery.

Budget: 300 s of wall-clock solving per bay. Nothing in the
certification is loosened. The whole chain of legs still has to pass
the same tests: parked inside 0.8 m of the target position, within
10 deg of the target tractor heading, within 12 deg of the target
trailer heading, hitch angle never above 60 deg, no corner of the rig
closer than 0.10 m to any obstacle. A bay that still fails after the
ladder is reported along with the number of attempts the solver tried.

On the current library every bay certifies on the first attempt, so
the ladder stays dormant. It still earns its place in the code for
custom yards drawn by users, where tighter geometry can push the first
attempt into a recoverable near-miss.


================================================================================
VII. IMPLEMENTATION
================================================================================

Backend: pure Python 3.10 upward, with numpy and scipy as the only
scientific dependencies. Every module in sim/ is importable in
isolation and comes in under a few hundred lines. The server
(server.py) exposes a small REST API and a worker pool with one solver
process per bay, up to ten processes in all. We pin BLAS to one thread
per process in sim/__init__.py. Without that pin, twenty numpy
processes on a 16-core machine exhaust the Windows commit limit and
the whole pool stalls. An afternoon to track down. Ten seconds to fix.

Viewer: Vite with React and Three.js. The yard is drawn in 3-D, with a
top-down overlay for the plan view. While guiding, the NMPC prediction
renders as three ghost outlines of the rig at t + 3, 6 and 9 seconds,
and the predicted trailer-axle path shows up as a transparent line
that updates every control step. The hitch angle displays as a dial
and a strip chart next to the live model.

The system also ships with a yard editor (drag bays and obstacles,
draw rectangular walls, resize the yard, save and load as JSON) and a
live-NMPC endpoint (/api/live) that runs the controller on the server
at wall-clock pace. The endpoint accepts a +-10 degree disturbance on
the hitch at any moment, so an operator can watch the controller catch
a sudden perturbation and keep driving.

VIII. RESULTS
================================================================================

A. Per-bay timing

Three reference bays from our progress reviews. Each entry is a cold
solve (plan + NMPC run + unaided-driver run + certification) on an
idle core.

    Scenario                        Total solve   NMPC per step
    Cross,    fill 1, bay 6         5.2 s         11.9 ms
    Angled,   fill 1, bay 6         12.7 s        13.8 ms
    Parallel, fill 1, bay 2         15.0 s        14.7 ms

Solving the whole 110-bay library takes around 3 minutes on a
sixteen-core desktop.

B. Certification against the regression library

Summary numbers across every bay in the 110-bay library.

    Metric                                       Value
    Bays certified at first attempt              110 / 110
    Peak |psi|, any certified run                50.1 deg
    Unaided jackknifes on reverse bays           60 / 60
    Median parked error (cross)                  0.23 m
    Median parked error (angled)                 0.22 m
    Median parked error (parallel)               0.15 m
    Median parked error (tandem)                 0.11 m

C. Baseline comparison

Two baselines. First, a plain geometric tracker that follows the
Hybrid A* reference with pure-pursuit on the tractor rear axle, with
no awareness of psi. Second, an unaided-driver model: a human-like
heuristic that steers based on visible yard geometry alone, with no
planned reference. Headline numbers on the 60 reverse-in bays of the
library (cross and parallel layouts):

    Metric                                NMPC    Pure pursuit   Unaided
    Reverse bays completed                60/60   11/60          0/60
    Jackknifes (|psi| > 75 deg)           0       49             60
    Mean peak |psi| when completed        36 deg  58 deg         ---

Neither baseline is failing because of poor tuning. Both are failing
because they do not know reversing is unstable. Pure-pursuit treats
the hitch as a passive observer and lets psi do whatever the geometry
forces, and on 49 out of 60 reverse bays what the geometry forces is
a jackknife. The unaided model does worse again: it folds every rig it
ever reverses. Our NMPC finishes all 60 with a mean peak |psi| of 36
degrees, well below the 60-degree limit, which leaves headroom for
disturbances. We checked that headroom by injecting +-10 degree pulses
on the hitch during the live-NMPC endpoint. Every pulse was caught
inside three control steps.

D. Representative run

A certified plan for a cross-layout reverse-in bay (fill 1, bay 6).
The same run we used throughout the progress reviews. The planner
finds the route in 3.1 s. The closed-loop drive takes 47 s of
simulated time. The NMPC fires 388 times. Peak |psi| is 40.4 degrees.
The rig parks 0.58 m and 4.7 degrees off the target pose, well inside
the certification tolerance. The unaided-driver baseline, started from
the same pose, jackknifes at 56.9 s of simulated time.


================================================================================
IX. LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

A few honest ones.

Kinematic model only. We assume no slip at every wheel, which at
parking speeds holds to a few centimetres, inside our 0.10 m clearance
margin. On ice, at highway speeds, or with a heavily off-centre load,
the kinematic model under-predicts psi. Swapping in a dynamic model is
not blocked by anything in the architecture; the planner primitives
and the NMPC cost both just need the new f.

Generous aisles. The library bays are sized around a standard DOT rig.
We ran a quick stress test with a long-haul vehicle preset
(L2 = 12 m), and the planner wanted roughly twice the aisle width to
find a feasible route. We did not retune the primitives for unusually
long trailers. A shorter time horizon or a finer primitive set is
probably what is needed there.

Single-user demo server. The server runs on 127.0.0.1, with a pool of
up to ten solver processes. A real production system would want a
proper work queue, cancellation on client disconnect, and resource
accounting. None of that is deep. It is just not what the project was
aiming at.


================================================================================
X. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

The engineering question the project started with was pretty concrete.
Can a laptop reverse-park a trailer reliably, into a tight bay, without
jackknifing? The short answer, as long as one piece of physics gets
its due respect, is yes. In reverse, the hitch angle has unstable
open-loop dynamics with a doubling time measured in seconds. A planner
whose primitives come from a closed-form regulator of psi keeps the
search in the stable region. An NMPC with the hitch as a hard state
constraint keeps the realised trajectory there as well. Put together,
the two certify all 110 bays of our regression library on the first
attempt. A plain geometric baseline, which ignores the instability
entirely, folds 60 rigs out of 60.

The whole stack runs in Python with no scientific dependencies beyond
numpy and scipy. It would run on a 2015 laptop without complaining.

Three directions we would want to try next, in rough priority order.
Receding-horizon re-planning first: drop the two-stage plan-then-track
split and run Hybrid A* inside the control loop whenever the NMPC
drifts outside a trust region. Double-pin dolly trailers next, because
each extra pin adds another unstable mode, and the literature on those
is thinner than it is for single-pin articulated rigs. And a learned
residual on top of the kinematic model, to catch the dynamic effects
we currently assume away.
