2What Your Part Must Achieve

Person B ends with: "reversing is unstable, so a controller must look ahead". You answer: here is how we decide where to go (Hybrid A*), and here is how we steer to get there safely (NMPC). For each algorithm, the professor asked for two things: what it is in general, and how it ties to our project. Leave him with three ideas:

4Graph Search and A*, From Scratch

Path planning as a search: cut the world into nodes (for a grid, each free cell), connect neighbours with edges that have a cost (length), and find the cheapest chain of edges from start S to goal G.

4.1 The Algorithm

A* keeps an open list of nodes waiting to be expanded, ordered by

$$f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$$
  • $g(n)$: the cost of the best path found so far from S to $n$ (known exactly).
  • $h(n)$: the heuristic, an estimate of the cost still to go from $n$ to G.
  • $f(n)$: estimated total cost of the best path through $n$.
1

Put S on the open list with $g = 0$.

2

Pop the node with the smallest $f$. If it is G, stop and trace the path back through parent links.

3

Otherwise mark it closed and, for each neighbour, compute $g_{\text{new}} = g(n) + \text{edge cost}$. If that beats the neighbour's best $g$ so far, record $n$ as its parent and push it with $f = g_{\text{new}} + h$.

4

Repeat from step 2.

4.2 The Heuristic

If $h$ never overestimates the true remaining cost (it is admissible), A* returns the cheapest path. If $h = 0$ it becomes Dijkstra's algorithm: correct but slow. A good heuristic is as close to the true cost as possible without exceeding it.

Weighted A* uses $f = g + \varepsilon h$ with $\varepsilon > 1$. It is greedier, expands far fewer nodes, and its path costs at most $\varepsilon$ times the optimum. We use $\varepsilon = 3$: speed matters more to us than the last few metres of path length.

5Why Grid A* Fails for a Truck

  • A grid path has 90° corners; a 12 m rig cannot turn on the spot (minimum rear-axle radius 5.7 m).
  • Grid cells ignore heading and hitch angle, but where the truck can go next depends on both (nonholonomic, Person A).
  • It ignores reversing, gear changes and the 60° hitch limit.

6Hybrid A*: the Idea

Hybrid A* (Dolgov, Thrun et al., Stanford, for the DARPA Urban Challenge) keeps A*'s open list and $f = g + h$, but changes what a node and a move are:

Grid A*Hybrid A*
Node storesA cellAn exact continuous state; here $(x_1, y_1, \theta_0, \psi)$
MovesJump to a neighbouring cellSimulate a short drivable arc (a motion primitive) on the vehicle model
Grid used forEverythingOnly to spot duplicates: two states in the same cell count as the same node
ResultUndrivable cornersEvery path is drivable, because it was driven in simulation

Our cell size (for duplicate detection only): 0.5 m in position, 5° in $\theta_0$, 12° in $\psi$, plus the gear. Hybrid A* is resolution complete in practice: it finds a path if one exists at this resolution, but it is not guaranteed optimal, because snapping to cells discards some states.

The Analytic Shot

Arcs alone rarely land exactly on the goal pose. So, from nodes reasonably close to the bay, the planner also tries a direct shot: a Dubins path (shortest curve with bounded curvature) from the trailer axle to the bay, driven on the real model by a hitch controller. The shot is accepted only if it is collision-free and ends inside the bay (within 0.35 m laterally, 4° heading, 5° hitch). Often the search is: a few arcs to get lined up, then one shot backwards into the bay.

7Our Motion Primitives and the Hitch Regulator

A motion primitive for a car is "drive 2 m with the wheel at angle $\delta$". For our truck that fails in reverse: with a fixed wheel the hitch angle runs away (Person B), so a reverse arc would end in a jackknife after a few expansions. Instead, each primitive steers the hitch to a target angle $\psi_{\text{des}}$ with a small feedback law.

7.1 Deriving the Regulator (Feedback Linearisation)

We choose how we want the hitch to behave: decay towards the target at a rate $k$ per metre, in either gear:

$$\dot\psi = -|v|\,k\,(\psi - \psi_{\text{des}})$$

Person B's factored hitch equation is $\dot\psi = v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1}\left(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi\right) - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}\right]$. Set the two equal and solve for $\tan\delta$ (note $|v| = v\,\mathrm{sign}(v)$):

$$\tan\delta = L_1\,\frac{\dfrac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \mathrm{sign}(v)\,k\,(\psi - \psi_{\text{des}})}{1 - \dfrac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi}$$

Check by substituting back: the bracket becomes $\frac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \mathrm{sign}(v)k(\psi - \psi_{\text{des}}) - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}$, the unstable $\sin\psi$ term cancels exactly, and $\dot\psi = -v\,\mathrm{sign}(v)\,k(\psi-\psi_{\text{des}}) = -|v|k(\psi - \psi_{\text{des}})$. ✓

With $k = 0.5$ per metre the error shrinks ×e every 2 m, forward or reverse. The steering is clipped to 85 % of the lock (29.75°) so the controller keeps some margin.

7.2 The Ten Primitives

  • 2 gears × 5 hitch targets $\psi_{\text{des}} \in \{0°, \pm 25°, \pm 45°\}$ = 10 arcs per node.
  • Each arc is 2 m long, integrated with RK4 in 4 steps of 0.5 m.
  • An arc is discarded if the hitch exceeds 52° (a margin below the 60° limit) or any point of either body comes within 0.35 m of an obstacle.

8Our Planner's Cost and Heuristic

8.1 Cost of One Arc (Adds to $g$)

$$c = \underbrace{2\,\text{m}\times\begin{cases}1.0 & \text{forward}\\ 1.4 & \text{reverse}\end{cases}}_{\text{distance}} + \underbrace{0.25\,|\psi_{\text{des}}|\times 2}_{\text{sharp turns}} + \underbrace{0.6\,|\psi_{\text{des}} - \psi_{\text{des}}^{\text{prev}}|}_{\text{changing turns}} + \underbrace{0.6\,\frac{|\psi_{\text{end}}|}{52°}\times 2}_{\text{bent hitch}} + \underbrace{6}_{\text{if gear changes}}$$

(angles in radians). Read it as preferences: shorter is better; reversing costs 40 % more than driving forward; gentle, steady turns beat sharp or changing ones; a straight hitch beats a bent one; every gear change costs as much as 6 m of driving. The planner then finds the route with the lowest total.

8.2 The Heuristic

$$h = 3\times\Big(\max\big(d_{\text{Dubins}},\ d_{\text{grid}}\big) + 3\,|\psi|\Big)$$
  • $d_{\text{Dubins}}$: length of the shortest Dubins path of the trailer axle to its parked pose, with turning radius 10 m. Why the trailer axle? It is a flat output: its path curvature $\kappa$ alone fixes the hitch angle, $\psi = \arctan(L_2\kappa)$. So the trailer axle moves like a car whose "steering" is $\psi$, and a 10 m radius corresponds to $\psi = \arctan(8/10) = 38.7°$.
  • $d_{\text{grid}}$: shortest distance to the bay around obstacles on a 0.5 m grid (ignores heading). The max of the two is a better estimate than either.
  • $3|\psi|$: a bent hitch still has to be straightened.
  • The factor 3 is the weighted-A* $\varepsilon$: plans in about 0.1 to 6 s instead of minutes.

9Model Predictive Control, From Scratch

MPC is control by repeated planning over a short horizon. At every control step:

1

Measure the current state $X_0$.

2

Predict: for any candidate sequence of future inputs, simulate the model $N$ steps ahead.

3

Optimise: find the input sequence that minimises a cost $J$ while satisfying constraints.

4

Apply only the first input, wait one control period, and go back to step 1. The horizon slides forward each time: a receding horizon.

"Nonlinear" MPC means step 2 uses the full nonlinear model (Person B's equations), not a linear approximation. The general problem is

$$\min_{u_1,\dots,u_M}\ J(X, u)\quad\text{subject to}\quad X_{k+1} = f(X_k, u_k),\quad g(X_k, u_k) \le 0$$
SAY IT LIKE THIS

"MPC is like a chess player: it thinks several moves ahead, picks the best line, plays only the first move, then thinks again after seeing the reply. Because it looks ahead, it sees a jackknife coming while there is still time to steer away."

Why corrections around the plan? The plan was driven on the same model, so its steering $\delta^{\text{ff}}$ is already nearly right. $u = 0$ means "follow the plan exactly"; the optimiser only has to find small corrections for drift and disturbances. Why 8 blocks instead of 24 values? Fewer variables solve much faster, and a driver could not follow a steering wheel that changes every 0.4 s anyway.

11The Cost Function, Term by Term

This is the part to understand best. The slide shows the compact form:

$$J = \sum_{i=1}^{24}\big\lVert X_i - X_i^{\text{ref}}\big\rVert_Q^2 \;+\; \sum_{j=1}^{8}\Big(R\,u_j^2 + S\,(u_j - u_{j-1})^2\Big)$$

It has three ideas: tracking (stay on the plan), effort (do not correct more than needed) and smoothness (do not jerk the wheel). The optimiser looks for the 8 corrections that make $J$ as small as possible.

11.1 What $\lVert e\rVert_Q^2$ Means

For an error vector $e$ and a diagonal weight matrix $Q = \mathrm{diag}(q_1, q_2, \dots)$:

$$\lVert e\rVert_Q^2 = e^T Q\,e = q_1e_1^2 + q_2e_2^2 + \cdots$$

It is a weighted sum of squared errors. Each weight $q_i$ says how much we care about that error compared with the others. Example: $e = (0.3, 0.05)$ and $Q = \mathrm{diag}(0.6, 15)$ give $0.6\times 0.09 + 15\times 0.0025 = 0.054 + 0.0375 = 0.0915$.

11.2 Which Errors We Track

Error at prediction step $i$UnitsMeaning
$e_{p_2}$: trailer-axle position minus plannedmWhere the back of the rig is compared with the plan (a 2-D vector, both $x$ and $y$)
$e_{\theta_1}$: trailer heading minus plannedradIs the trailer pointing the planned way?
$e_{p_1}$: tractor rear-axle position minus plannedmWhere the cab is compared with the plan
$e_{\theta_0}$: tractor heading minus plannedradIs the cab pointing the planned way?
$e_\psi$: hitch angle minus plannedradIs the rig bent as much as planned, no more?

Angle errors are wrapped into $(-180°, 180°]$ so that 359° and 1° count as 2° apart.

11.3 The Full Formula With Our Real Weights

Written out exactly as the code computes it (all angles in radians):

$$\begin{aligned} J \;=\; &\sum_{i=1}^{24}\Big(\,0.6\,\lVert e_{p_2,i}\rVert^2 + 1.5\,e_{\theta_1,i}^2 + 0.15\,\lVert e_{p_1,i}\rVert^2 + 0.3\,e_{\theta_0,i}^2 + 15\,e_{\psi,i}^2\Big) && \text{stage cost}\\ +\;&\Big(\,8\,\lVert e_{p_2,24}\rVert^2 + 60\,e_{\theta_1,24}^2 + 1.0\,\lVert e_{p_1,24}\rVert^2 + 60\,e_{\psi,24}^2\Big) && \text{terminal cost}\\ +\;&0.02\sum_{j=1}^{8}u_j^2 && \text{effort } (R)\\ +\;&3\sum_{j=1}^{8}\big(u_j - u_{j-1}\big)^2 && \text{smoothness } (S) \end{aligned}$$

Here $u_0$ is the correction that was applied in the previous solve, so the first change is also measured against what the wheel is doing now.

11.4 Why Each Weight Is What It Is

Term and weightReason
Trailer axle position 0.6, heading 1.5The main target. The trailer axle is the flat output and the part that must end up in the bay. Tracking the tractor's axle instead fails in reverse, because the tractor first moves the wrong way (non-minimum phase, Person B Section 10).
Hitch angle 15The largest stage weight. Staying close to the planned hitch angle keeps the rig away from the unstable region; when forced to choose, we prefer a straighter rig to a slightly tighter path.
Tractor position 0.15, heading 0.3Small: keep the cab sensible, but let it deviate if that helps the trailer.

Effort $R$ = 0.02Tiny: corrections are cheap, because the plan's steering may need real adjustment. It only breaks ties in favour of "follow the plan".
Smoothness $S$ = 3Large compared with $R$: change the wheel gradually, so a human driver can follow and the steering (limited to 45°/s) can keep up.

11.5 Why Squares?

  • Sign does not matter: 0.3 m left and 0.3 m right are equally bad.
  • Big errors cost much more than small ones: twice the error, four times the cost. The controller fixes large deviations first and tolerates tiny ones.
  • Smooth: a quadratic has a gradient everywhere, which the solver (SQP, Section 13) needs, and near the optimum the problem looks like a bowl, so it converges in a few iterations.

11.6 Worked Example: One Prediction Step

Suppose at one step the trailer axle is 0.3 m off the plan, the trailer and tractor headings are 2° off, the tractor axle is 0.3 m off, and the hitch is 3° off. In radians, $2° = 0.0349$ and $3° = 0.0524$.

Reading it: 0.3 m of trailer error and 3° of hitch error cost about the same (0.054 and 0.041). That is the balance the weights were tuned for: metres and radians are different units, and the weights make them comparable. Heading errors of 2° are cheap on their own; they matter because they turn into position errors a few steps later, which the cost then sees.

Effort and smoothness in the same units: a 5° correction ($0.0873$ rad) costs $0.02\times 0.0873^2 = 0.00015$: almost free. Changing the correction by 5° from one block to the next costs $3\times 0.0873^2 = 0.023$, about the same as a 2.2° hitch error held for one step. So the controller corrects readily, but spreads a large correction over several blocks.

12Hard Constraints Versus Cost

Some things must never happen, whatever the cost says. These are hard constraints, checked at every one of the 24 predicted steps:

$$|\psi_i| \le 60° \qquad \text{clearance}_i \ge 0.25\ \text{m} \qquad |u_j| \le 35°,\ \ \delta_i \text{ clipped to } \pm 35°$$

Why not just a big penalty? A penalty can always be outweighed: if tracking the path badly enough costs more than the penalty, the optimiser would accept a 65° hitch. A constraint cannot be traded away. In our tests the hitch never exceeded 50.1°, against the 60° limit. (Our benchmark also ran the NMPC with the hitch limit as a soft cost only, to show the difference.)

In the code the hitch limit is written as 48 inequalities ($60° - \psi_i \ge 0$ and $60° + \psi_i \ge 0$ for each step), and clearance as 24 inequalities using a signed-distance map of the yard over both bodies.

13How the Problem Is Solved

  • Single shooting: only the 8 corrections are variables. For any guess, the predicted states come from simulating the model, so the dynamics are satisfied automatically.
  • SLSQP (Sequential Least-Squares Quadratic Programming, from SciPy): each iteration approximates the cost by a quadratic and the constraints by straight lines, solves that small problem, and moves towards its answer. It repeats until it stops improving (at most 40 iterations).
  • Gradients by finite differences: nudge each of the 8 corrections slightly and re-simulate. We simulate all 9 versions in one batched computation, which is why a solve takes 19 to 42 ms, far inside the 250 ms control period.
  • Warm start: each solve starts from the previous solution, which is usually almost right.
  • Fallbacks: if no solution satisfies everything, try again keeping only the hitch limit hard (a rig that cannot both dodge and stay unfolded must stay unfolded). If that fails too, the stable hitch regulator from Section 7 steers for that step.
  • Horizon stops at the segment end: the prediction holds still where the planned segment ends (at a gear change or in the bay), so a wall behind the bay is not mistaken for a collision the rig would never reach.

14How the Two Work Together

  1. The user picks a free bay. Hybrid A* plans the route once: forward set-up, stop, reverse into the bay.
  2. The route is cut at gear changes into segments, each with a planned steering $\delta^{\text{ff}}$ and speed profile.
  3. Every 0.25 s the NMPC solves for the steering correction, with the hitch limit hard.
  4. The guidance layer turns the steering into wheel turns, km/h and distance for the driver.
  5. Near the bay the final approach is re-planned from the actual state every 8 m, so small errors never accumulate.

16Questions the Professor May Ask

What is the difference between A* and Dijkstra?
Dijkstra orders by $g$ only; A* by $g + h$. A good heuristic focuses the search towards the goal. With $h = 0$ they are the same.
Is your heuristic admissible? Is the path optimal?
No and no, deliberately. We multiply it by 3 (weighted A*) to plan in seconds instead of minutes, which gives up the optimality guarantee; in practice the routes are close to the shortest. What we do guarantee is that every route is drivable and collision-free, because it was simulated and checked.
Why Hybrid A* and not RRT?
Hybrid A* is deterministic (same yard, same plan), handles a cost (prefer forward, few gear changes) naturally, and works well in structured spaces like parking yards. RRT is random and usually needs smoothing.
Why do the primitives use a hitch regulator?
With a fixed steering angle, reverse arcs are unstable and jackknife within a couple of expansions. The regulator cancels the unstable term (feedback linearisation) so the hitch converges to its target in both gears.
Why is the trailer axle used in the heuristic and the cost?
It is a flat output: its curvature fixes $\psi$, so it behaves like a car steered by $\psi$. And in reverse the tractor's axle is non-minimum phase; tracking it makes the controller steer the wrong way first.
Why NMPC rather than PID or LQR?
It handles hard constraints, uses the full nonlinear model, and looks ahead, which the unstable reverse dynamics need. PID only reacts; LQR has no constraints and is linear.
How did you choose the 9.6 s horizon?
The hitch error grows ×e every 8 m in reverse; at reversing speed that takes several seconds. The horizon must be long enough to see the growth coming while there is still time to act; a much shorter horizon sees the jackknife too late.
What do $Q$, $R$, $S$ do?
$Q$ weights tracking errors (trailer axle and hitch most), $R$ penalises correction size (small), $S$ penalises changes of correction (large, for smooth steering). Only their ratios matter.
Why a terminal cost?
So the predicted motion must end on the plan; otherwise the optimiser could push an error just past the horizon, where in reverse it would grow.
Is the NMPC guaranteed stable?
We have no formal proof (no terminal set). We certify empirically: every bay is driven in closed loop and checked before it is offered, and pushes of ±10° on the hitch are caught by the live controller.
What if SLSQP fails?
Retry with only the hitch limit hard; if still infeasible, the hitch regulator steers. In the 110-bay tests the hitch limit was never broken.
Why only 8 decision variables?
Fewer variables solve far faster (we need under 250 ms), and steering held 1.2 s at a time is something a human driver can actually follow.
