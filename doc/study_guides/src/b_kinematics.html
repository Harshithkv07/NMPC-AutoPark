2What Your Part Must Achieve

Person A gives the professor the symbols: state $X = [x_1, y_1, \theta_0, \psi]^T$, input $U = [v, \delta]^T$. You give him the four equations $\dot X = f(X, U)$, show where each comes from, and end with the single most important fact of the project:

YOUR HEADLINE

Linearising the hitch equation gives $\dot\psi \approx -\frac{v}{L_2}\psi$. The eigenvalue $\lambda = -v/L_2$ is negative going forward (stable) and positive in reverse (unstable): a 2° error grows to 60° in 28 m. That is why Part 3 needs a planner and a predictive controller.

The four equations you will derive:

$$\begin{aligned} \dot x_1 &= v\cos\theta_0\\ \dot y_1 &= v\sin\theta_0\\ \dot\theta_0 &= \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta\\ \dot\psi &= v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1} - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \frac{d\,\tan\delta\,\cos\psi}{L_1L_2}\right] \end{aligned}$$

3The Maths Toolkit

Everything in this guide uses only the tools below. Learn them first; the derivations are then just bookkeeping.

3.1 Heading and Sideways Unit Vectors

$$\hat u(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \hat n(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$$

Both have length 1 and are perpendicular: $\hat u\cdot\hat n = 0$.

3.2 Differentiating a Rotating Unit Vector

If the heading changes with time, $\theta = \theta(t)$, the chain rule gives

$$\frac{d}{dt}\hat u(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta\\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\dot\theta = \dot\theta\,\hat n(\theta)$$

Meaning: when a body rotates at rate $\dot\theta$, a point a distance $r$ along its axis moves sideways at speed $r\dot\theta$. This one rule produces every rotation term in this guide.

3.3 Dot Products Between Two Headings

The dot product picks out the component of one vector along another. Using $\cos(a-b) = \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b$ and $\sin(a-b) = \sin a\cos b - \cos a\sin b$:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat u(a)\cdot\hat u(b) &= \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b = \cos(a-b)\\ \hat u(a)\cdot\hat n(b) &= -\cos a\sin b + \sin a\cos b = \sin(a-b)\\ \hat n(a)\cdot\hat n(b) &= \sin a\sin b + \cos a\cos b = \cos(a-b) \end{aligned}$$

With $a = \theta_0$ and $b = \theta_1$, the difference $a - b$ is exactly the hitch angle $\psi$. That is why $\sin\psi$ and $\cos\psi$ appear in the trailer equation.

3.4 Small-Angle Approximations

For an angle $x$ in radians close to 0: $\sin x \approx x$, $\cos x \approx 1$, $\tan x \approx x$. (At 10° the error of $\sin x \approx x$ is only 0.5 %.) Always use radians in formulas: $1° = \pi/180 \approx 0.01745$ rad.

3.5 The Exponential Solution

The equation $\dot\psi = \lambda\psi$ has the solution $\psi(t) = \psi_0\,e^{\lambda t}$. If $\lambda < 0$, $\psi$ dies out; if $\lambda > 0$, it grows, multiplying by $e \approx 2.72$ every $1/\lambda$ seconds and doubling every $\ln 2/\lambda$ seconds.

4The Tractor: Kinematic Bicycle Model

4.1 The Bicycle Simplification

A real tractor has two front wheels and two (or four) rear wheels. If the steering is designed properly (Ackermann geometry), all wheels turn about one common point, and the left and right wheels of an axle behave like a single wheel at the axle's centre. So we draw the tractor as a bicycle: one rear wheel, one steered front wheel, $L_1$ apart.

4.2 Rear Axle: the First Two Equations

The rear wheel cannot slide sideways (Person A's no-slip constraint), so its velocity points exactly along the heading:

$$\dot p_1 = v\,\hat u(\theta_0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot x_1 = v\cos\theta_0,\qquad \dot y_1 = v\sin\theta_0$$

This is also the definition of $v$: the signed speed of the rear-axle centre, negative in reverse.

4.3 Front Axle: the Turning-Rate Equation, by Vectors

1

Front-axle position: $p_f = p_1 + L_1\,\hat u(\theta_0)$.

2

Differentiate, using rule 3.2:

$$\dot p_f = v\,\hat u(\theta_0) + L_1\dot\theta_0\,\hat n(\theta_0)$$
3

The front wheel points at $\theta_0 + \delta$ and cannot slide sideways, so the velocity has no component along $\hat n(\theta_0+\delta)$:

$$\dot p_f\cdot\hat n(\theta_0+\delta) = 0$$
4

Use the dot products of 3.3 with $a = \theta_0$, $b = \theta_0 + \delta$, so $a - b = -\delta$:

$$\hat u(\theta_0)\cdot\hat n(\theta_0+\delta) = \sin(-\delta) = -\sin\delta,\qquad \hat n(\theta_0)\cdot\hat n(\theta_0+\delta) = \cos\delta$$
5

Substitute:

$$-v\sin\delta + L_1\dot\theta_0\cos\delta = 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\dot\theta_0 = \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta}$$

4.4 The Same Result from the ICR Geometry

Every wheel rolls on a circle around the ICR, so each wheel's axle line passes through it. The rear axle line is perpendicular to the body; the front axle line is perpendicular to the front wheel. In Figure 2 this makes a right-angled triangle with the body $L_1$ as one side and the turning radius $R$ (rear axle to ICR) as the other, and the angle at the ICR equals $\delta$ (two lines each turned by $\delta$ make the same angle). So

$$\tan\delta = \frac{L_1}{R}\ \Longrightarrow\ R = \frac{L_1}{\tan\delta},\qquad \dot\theta_0 = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta$$

The second step is the rule for any circle: angular rate = speed ÷ radius. Both methods agree; use whichever you can draw faster on a board.

Numbers: at full lock, $\delta = 35°$: $R = 4/\tan 35° = 4/0.700 = 5.71$ m, the tightest circle the rear axle can drive. At $\delta = 0$: $R \to \infty$, straight line, $\dot\theta_0 = 0$.

5The Trailer: Hitch and No-Slip

1

Hitch position and velocity. The hitch is $d$ ahead of the rear axle on the tractor:

$$p_h = p_1 + d\,\hat u(\theta_0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot p_h = v\,\hat u(\theta_0) + d\,\dot\theta_0\,\hat n(\theta_0)$$
2

Trailer-axle position and velocity. The trailer axle is $L_2$ behind the hitch along the trailer:

$$p_2 = p_h - L_2\,\hat u(\theta_1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot p_2 = \dot p_h - L_2\,\dot\theta_1\,\hat n(\theta_1)$$
3

No-slip at the trailer axle: no velocity along the trailer's sideways direction:

$$\dot p_2\cdot\hat n(\theta_1) = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \dot p_h\cdot\hat n(\theta_1) - L_2\,\dot\theta_1\underbrace{\hat n(\theta_1)\cdot\hat n(\theta_1)}_{=1} = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \dot\theta_1 = \frac{\dot p_h\cdot\hat n(\theta_1)}{L_2}$$

The numerator is exactly $v_\perp$ in Figure 4: the sideways part of the hitch velocity.

4

Compute $v_\perp$ with 3.3, $a = \theta_0$, $b = \theta_1$, $a - b = \psi$:

$$v_\perp = v\,\underbrace{\hat u(\theta_0)\cdot\hat n(\theta_1)}_{\sin\psi} + d\,\dot\theta_0\,\underbrace{\hat n(\theta_0)\cdot\hat n(\theta_1)}_{\cos\psi} = v\sin\psi + d\,\dot\theta_0\cos\psi$$
5

Result:

$$\boxed{\dot\theta_1 = \frac{v\sin\psi + d\,\dot\theta_0\cos\psi}{L_2}}$$
SAY IT LIKE THIS

"The trailer is dragged by its hitch. Split the hitch velocity into a part along the trailer and a part across it. The trailer axle can only roll, so the along part just pulls the trailer straight, and the across part swings it about its axle, with lever arm $L_2$. That gives $\dot\theta_1 = v_\perp/L_2$."

Extra, if asked: the trailer axle's own speed is the along part, $v_2 = \dot p_h\cdot\hat u(\theta_1) = v\cos\psi - d\,\dot\theta_0\sin\psi$. For a straight rig ($\psi = 0$) it equals $v$, as expected.

6The Hitch-Angle Equation

6.1 Assemble It

Since $\psi = \theta_0 - \theta_1$, its rate is $\dot\psi = \dot\theta_0 - \dot\theta_1$. Substitute $\dot\theta_0 = \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta$ into both places:

$$\dot\psi = \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta - \frac{v\sin\psi}{L_2} - \frac{d\cos\psi}{L_2}\cdot\frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta = v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1} - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \frac{d\,\tan\delta\,\cos\psi}{L_1L_2}\right]$$

A useful factored form (group the two steering terms):

$$\dot\psi = v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1}\left(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi\right) - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}\right]$$

6.2 What Each Term Means

7The Complete Model, and How We Simulate It

7.1 Every Term Is Proportional to $v$

Look at the four equations: each right-hand side has a factor $v$. Divide by $v$ and write $ds = v\,dt$ for the (signed) distance travelled:

$$\frac{dx_1}{ds} = \cos\theta_0,\quad \frac{dy_1}{ds} = \sin\theta_0,\quad \frac{d\theta_0}{ds} = \frac{\tan\delta}{L_1},\quad \frac{d\psi}{ds} = \frac{\tan\delta}{L_1}\left(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi\right) - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}$$

Consequence: the shape of the path depends only on the steering along the distance, not on how fast you drive. Driving the same steering twice as fast traces the same path in half the time. That is why the planner chooses the path first and a speed profile separately.

7.2 Integrating with RK4

The equations give rates; to predict the future we integrate them in small time steps $\Delta t$. We use the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (RK4), holding $v$ and $\delta$ constant over each step:

$$\begin{aligned} k_1 &= f(X_k), & k_2 &= f\!\left(X_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k_1\right),\\ k_3 &= f\!\left(X_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k_2\right), & k_4 &= f(X_k + \Delta t\,k_3),\\ X_{k+1} &= X_k + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}\left(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4\right) \end{aligned}$$

It samples the slope four times per step and averages them, so its error per step shrinks like $\Delta t^5$ (Euler's method, which uses only $k_1$, shrinks like $\Delta t^2$). We use 0.05 s steps in the simulator, 0.4 s steps inside the NMPC prediction and 0.5 m steps in the planner's motion primitives. One model, one integrator, shared by planner, controller and viewer.

8Why Reversing Is Unstable

8.1 Linearise Around the Straight Rig

Hold the steering straight, $\delta = 0$. The hitch equation becomes

$$\dot\psi = -\frac{v}{L_2}\sin\psi$$

Its equilibrium is $\psi = 0$. Near it, $\sin\psi \approx \psi$:

$$\dot\psi \approx \lambda\,\psi,\qquad \lambda = -\frac{v}{L_2},\qquad \psi(t) = \psi_0\,e^{\lambda t}$$
GearEigenvalueBehaviour (at 1 m/s, $L_2$ = 8 m)
Forward, $v > 0$$\lambda = -v/L_2 < 0$Stable. Any hitch angle decays with time constant $L_2/v$ = 8 s. A towed trailer straightens itself.
Reverse, $v < 0$$\lambda = |v|/L_2 > 0$Unstable. The angle grows ×e every 8 m, doubles every $L_2\ln 2 = 5.5$ m.

With steering included, the linearisation is $\dot\psi \approx -\frac{v}{L_2}\psi + \frac{v}{L_1}\left(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\right)\delta$: the first term is the instability, the second is how steering pushes on it.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

"The hitch term is minus $v$ over $L_2$ times $\psi$. Going forward it pulls $\psi$ back to zero, like a trailer following the truck. In reverse $v$ is negative, the sign flips, and the term pushes $\psi$ away from zero, like balancing a broom on your hand. The eigenvalue is positive, so any small error grows exponentially."

8.2 The Exact Solution (Not Only Near Zero)

We do not need the approximation. Write the equation in distance: reversing, $ds = |v|\,dt$ and $v = -|v|$, so

$$\frac{d\psi}{ds} = \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int\frac{d\psi}{\sin\psi} = \int\frac{ds}{L_2}$$

The standard integral is $\int\frac{d\psi}{\sin\psi} = \ln\left|\tan\frac{\psi}{2}\right|$. You can check it by differentiating, using $\sin\psi = 2\sin\frac{\psi}{2}\cos\frac{\psi}{2}$:

$$\frac{d}{d\psi}\ln\tan\frac{\psi}{2} = \frac{1}{\tan\frac{\psi}{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\cos^2\frac{\psi}{2}} = \frac{1}{2\sin\frac{\psi}{2}\cos\frac{\psi}{2}} = \frac{1}{\sin\psi}$$

So

$$\ln\tan\frac{\psi}{2} - \ln\tan\frac{\psi_0}{2} = \frac{s}{L_2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\tan\frac{\psi}{2} = \tan\frac{\psi_0}{2}\;e^{\,s/L_2}}\qquad\text{(reverse)}$$

Forward, the exponent is $-s/L_2$ and the angle decays. For small angles, $\tan(\psi/2) \approx \psi/2$ and this becomes $\psi \approx \psi_0 e^{s/L_2}$, the linear result again.

8.3 The Numbers on the Slide

Solve for the distance: $s = L_2\ln\dfrac{\tan(\psi/2)}{\tan(\psi_0/2)}$. Starting from a 2° error ($\psi_0/2 = 1°$):

ReachCalculationDistance reversed
60° (our hard limit)$8\ln\dfrac{\tan 30°}{\tan 1°} = 8\ln\dfrac{0.5774}{0.01746} = 8\ln 33.08 = 8 \times 3.499$28.0 m
75° (physical jackknife)$8\ln\dfrac{\tan 37.5°}{\tan 1°} = 8\ln 43.96 = 8 \times 3.783$30.3 m

Why this matters: a bay approach is 20 to 40 m of reversing. Without correction, any small error becomes a jackknife within one manoeuvre. A controller must act early, before the error grows, which means it must look ahead: that is the motivation for NMPC in Part 3.

9How Much Steering Holds a Hitch Angle

To hold the hitch at a constant angle (for example while reversing round a curve), set $\dot\psi = 0$ in the factored equation (with $v \ne 0$):

$$\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1}\left(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi\right) = \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\delta_{\text{hold}} = \frac{L_1}{L_2}\cdot\frac{\sin\psi}{1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi}$$

At $\psi$ = 60°: $\tan\delta = 0.5 \times \dfrac{0.866}{1 - 0.0625 \times 0.5} = \dfrac{0.433}{0.969} = 0.447$, so $\delta_{\text{hold}} = 24.1°$.

The largest angle steering can hold. Let $c = d/L_2 = 0.0625$ and $g(\psi) = \dfrac{\sin\psi}{1 - c\cos\psi}$. Differentiate (quotient rule):

$$g'(\psi) = \frac{\cos\psi\,(1 - c\cos\psi) - \sin\psi\,(c\sin\psi)}{(1 - c\cos\psi)^2} = \frac{\cos\psi - c}{(1 - c\cos\psi)^2}$$

(using $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$). It is zero at $\cos\psi^* = c$, so $\psi^* = 86.4°$, where $g = 1/\sqrt{1 - c^2} = 1.002$. Then $\tan\delta_{\max} = 0.5 \times 1.002$, so $\delta = 26.6°$.

WHY THE LIMIT IS 60°

To straighten a hitch you must steer more than the holding angle. At 60° holding needs 24.1°, leaving about 11° of lock to pull the trailer back. Much beyond 60° there is too little margin, and past about 86° no steering can even hold the angle. So the controller treats 60° as a hard limit and the planner stays below 52°.

10Why Reversing Feels Backwards

Drivers learn "to reverse the trailer left, first steer right". The equations show why. Reverse ($v = -1$ m/s) with constant left steering $\delta = +10°$ from a straight rig:

  • $\dot\theta_0 = v\tan\delta/L_1 < 0$: the tractor heading turns clockwise straight away.
  • That makes $\psi$ negative, and then $\dot\theta_1 \approx v\sin\psi/L_2 > 0$: the trailer heading turns the other way.

In control language, the tractor's rear axle is a non-minimum-phase output in reverse: to move the trailer one way, the tractor must first move the other way. A controller that only reacts to the current error (like PID) steers the wrong way at first. This is why our NMPC tracks the trailer axle (Person C will mention it).

12The Derivation on a Board, in Six Lines

  1. $\hat u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $\hat n = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$, $\frac{d}{dt}\hat u = \dot\theta\,\hat n$.
  2. Rear axle rolls: $\dot p_1 = v\,\hat u_0$ ⇒ $\dot x_1 = v\cos\theta_0$, $\dot y_1 = v\sin\theta_0$.
  3. Front wheel rolls: $(v\hat u_0 + L_1\dot\theta_0\hat n_0)\cdot\hat n(\theta_0+\delta) = 0$ ⇒ $-v\sin\delta + L_1\dot\theta_0\cos\delta = 0$ ⇒ $\dot\theta_0 = v\tan\delta/L_1$.
  4. Hitch: $\dot p_h = v\hat u_0 + d\dot\theta_0\hat n_0$. Trailer axle rolls: $\dot p_2\cdot\hat n_1 = 0$ ⇒ $\dot\theta_1 = \dot p_h\cdot\hat n_1/L_2$.
  5. $\hat u_0\cdot\hat n_1 = \sin\psi$, $\hat n_0\cdot\hat n_1 = \cos\psi$ ⇒ $\dot\theta_1 = (v\sin\psi + d\dot\theta_0\cos\psi)/L_2$.
  6. $\dot\psi = \dot\theta_0 - \dot\theta_1 = v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1} - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \frac{d\tan\delta\cos\psi}{L_1L_2}\right]$; at $\delta = 0$, small $\psi$: $\dot\psi \approx -\frac{v}{L_2}\psi$.

14Questions the Professor May Ask

Derive $\dot\psi$ on the board.
Use the six lines in Section 12. Start from the no-slip constraints, not from the final formula.
Why does $\tan\delta$ appear and not $\delta$?
From the ICR triangle: $\tan\delta = L_1/R$. For small angles $\tan\delta \approx \delta$, but at 35° the difference is 14 %.
What is the eigenvalue and what does it mean?
$\lambda = -v/L_2$ from linearising at $\psi = 0$, $\delta = 0$. Its sign decides stability: negative forward, positive in reverse. $1/|\lambda|$ is the time for a ×e change: 8 s at 1 m/s.
Is the system stable forward?
The hitch angle is: a trailer straightens behind a truck driving forward. Position and heading are not "stable" in any sense; they just follow the steering.
Why does speed not appear in the 28 m figure?
Every term is proportional to $v$, so in terms of distance the growth is the same at any speed: ×e every $L_2$ metres. Faster reversing only makes it happen sooner in time.
What does the offset $d$ do?
With the hitch ahead of the rear axle, steering moves the hitch sideways, which slightly rotates the trailer the same way. It reduces the steering term by $(1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi)$, about 6 %.
Why 60° as the limit?
Holding 60° needs 24.1° of steering, leaving ~11° of our 35° lock to recover; beyond about 86° no steering can hold the angle. The physical jackknife is 75°.
What is non-minimum phase here?
In reverse, the tractor initially moves opposite to where the trailer must go (Figure 7). Reactive control steers the wrong way first; we track the trailer axle instead.
Why RK4 and not Euler?
Fourth-order accuracy at the 0.4 s prediction steps the NMPC uses; Euler would need much smaller steps for the same accuracy. RK4 costs four evaluations of a cheap function.
What if the wheels slip?
Then the no-slip constraints, and the model, break. At ≤ 2 m/s on dry asphalt slip is centimetres, inside our 0.25 m clearance. Ice or speed would need a dynamic tyre model.

The Model

$$\dot x_1 = v\cos\theta_0,\quad \dot y_1 = v\sin\theta_0,\quad \dot\theta_0 = \frac{v}{L_1}\tan\delta,\quad \dot\psi = v\left[\frac{\tan\delta}{L_1} - \frac{\sin\psi}{L_2} - \frac{d\tan\delta\cos\psi}{L_1L_2}\right]$$

Where Each Comes From

Rear axle rolls along its heading. Front wheel rolls along $\theta_0 + \delta$ ⇒ $R = L_1/\tan\delta$, $\dot\theta_0 = v/R$. Trailer axle rolls ⇒ $\dot\theta_1 = v_\perp/L_2$, $v_\perp = v\sin\psi + d\dot\theta_0\cos\psi$. Then $\dot\psi = \dot\theta_0 - \dot\theta_1$.

Tools

$\frac{d}{dt}\hat u = \dot\theta\hat n$; $\hat u(a)\cdot\hat n(b) = \sin(a-b)$; $\hat n(a)\cdot\hat n(b) = \cos(a-b)$.

Instability

$\delta = 0$, small $\psi$: $\dot\psi \approx -\frac{v}{L_2}\psi$, $\lambda = -v/L_2$. Forward stable, reverse unstable: ×e every 8 m, doubles every 5.5 m. Exact: $\tan\frac{\psi}{2} = \tan\frac{\psi_0}{2}e^{s/L_2}$; 2° → 60° in 28.0 m, → 75° in 30.3 m.

Holding and the Limit

$\tan\delta_{\text{hold}} = \frac{L_1}{L_2}\frac{\sin\psi}{1 - \frac{d}{L_2}\cos\psi}$: 24.1° at 60°, maximum 26.6° (at 86°). Limit 60° leaves ~11° of 35° lock to recover.

Numbers

$L_1$ = 4 m, $L_2$ = 8 m, $d$ = 0.5 m, $R_{\min}$ = 5.71 m at 35°. RK4: 0.05 s (simulation), 0.4 s (NMPC), 0.5 m (planner).
