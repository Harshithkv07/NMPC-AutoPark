2What Your Part Must Achieve

Your four minutes set up everything the others say. If the professor understands what the four state variables are and why the truck is nonholonomic, Parts 2 and 3 follow naturally. Aim to leave him with three ideas:

  1. The truck is a wheeled mobile robot: two rigid bodies (tractor and trailer) joined by one passive revolute joint (the hitch).
  2. Its wheels roll without slipping, which gives velocity constraints that cannot be integrated: the system is nonholonomic. It cannot move sideways, yet it can still reach any position and heading.
  3. We describe it with four states and two inputs: $X = [x_1, y_1, \theta_0, \psi]^T$ and $U = [v, \delta]^T$. We choose the hitch angle $\psi$ as a state because it is the quantity we must keep safe.

3What a Model Is, and Which Kind We Use

A model is two things: a short list of numbers that says where the robot is right now (the state), and a rule that says how those numbers change when we apply an input. Written generally:

$$\dot X = f(X,\, U)$$

The dot means "rate of change with time". Part 2 (Person B) derives the actual function $f$. Your job is to set up $X$, $U$ and the assumptions behind them.

Kinematic, Not Dynamic

There are two kinds of vehicle model:

Kinematic model (ours)Dynamic model
DescribesGeometry of motion: positions, angles, velocitiesForces and torques: engine, tyre forces, mass, inertia
Main assumptionWheels roll without slippingTyres slip a little; slip creates the force
Valid whenLow speed, good grip (parking, yards)Highway speed, hard braking, ice
InputsSpeed $v$ and steering angle $\delta$Throttle or brake force and steering torque

At yard speeds (at most 2 m/s, about 7 km/h) tyre slip is a few centimetres, smaller than our 0.25 m safety clearance. So the kinematic model is accurate enough and much simpler. Say this confidently if asked "why not a dynamic model?".

Our Assumptions, in One List

  • Motion is planar: a flat yard, so each body needs only $x$, $y$ and a heading angle.
  • Tractor and trailer are rigid bodies: they do not bend.
  • The hitch is a frictionless pin joint (revolute joint), placed $d$ = 0.5 m ahead of the tractor's rear axle.
  • Wheels roll without slipping sideways.
  • Each axle's two wheels act like one wheel at the axle centre (the "bicycle" simplification, Part 2).
  • One trailer, and the trailer has one axle (or a tandem group that behaves like one).

4Two Rigid Bodies and One Passive Joint

Counting Degrees of Freedom

A rigid body moving on a plane needs 3 numbers: $x$, $y$ and a heading angle. So two separate bodies would need 6. The hitch pins a point of the tractor to a point of the trailer; that removes 2 freedoms (the two bodies can no longer separate in $x$ or in $y$), but they can still rotate relative to each other. So:

$$\underbrace{3 + 3}_{\text{two free bodies}} \;-\; \underbrace{2}_{\text{revolute joint}} \;=\; 4 \text{ degrees of freedom}$$

This is the planar version of the Grübler (Kutzbach) count you saw for robot arms: each revolute joint removes 2 of the 3 planar freedoms.

Like a Two-Link Arm, With Two Differences

Two-link planar armTractor-trailer
LinksTwo rigid linksTwo rigid bodies
JointRevolute, driven by a motorRevolute, passive (no motor at the hitch)
BaseFixed to the groundMoves: the tractor drives around
Joint angle$q_2$Hitch angle $\psi$
How the joint angle changesYou command itOnly indirectly, through the wheels (this is why reversing is hard)
SAY IT LIKE THIS

"Think of the rig as a two-link robot arm lying flat on the ground, except the base drives around and the joint has no motor. The only way to change the hitch angle is to drive and steer the tractor."

5Frames and Rigid-Body Transforms

We attach a coordinate frame to each body: {T} at the tractor's rear-axle centre, pointing along the tractor; {R} at the trailer axle, pointing along the trailer. Knowing the tractor's pose, the rest of the rig follows by chaining transforms, exactly like forward kinematics of an arm.

Unit Vectors

For any heading $\theta$, define the unit vector along it and the one perpendicular to it (rotated 90° anticlockwise):

$$\hat u(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta \\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat n(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$$

The Chain: Rear Axle → Hitch → Trailer Axle

1

Rear axle (tractor frame origin): $p_1 = (x_1,\ y_1)$.

2

Hitch: $d$ metres ahead of the rear axle, along the tractor's heading:

$$p_h = p_1 + d\,\hat u(\theta_0)$$
3

Trailer axle: $L_2$ metres behind the hitch, along the trailer's heading:

$$p_2 = p_h - L_2\,\hat u(\theta_1), \qquad \theta_1 = \theta_0 - \psi$$

The Same Thing as a Homogeneous Transform

If the professor asks for the matrix form, the tractor's pose in the world is

$$T_{WT} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_0 & -\sin\theta_0 & x_1\\ \sin\theta_0 & \cos\theta_0 & y_1\\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_h = T_{WT}\begin{pmatrix} d\\ 0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}$$

Then rotate by $-\psi$ at the hitch and move $L_2$ backwards to reach the trailer axle: $T_{WR} = T_{WT}\,\mathrm{Trans}(d,0)\,\mathrm{Rot}(-\psi)\,\mathrm{Trans}(-L_2, 0)$. This is the same chain as a two-link arm: translate, rotate at the joint, translate along the second link.

Worked Example

Take $x_1 = 0$, $y_1 = 0$, $\theta_0 = 15°$, $\psi = 30°$ (the pose in the slide figure). Then $\theta_1 = 15° - 30° = -15°$ and

$p_h = (0.5\cos 15°,\ 0.5\sin 15°) = (0.483,\ 0.129)$ m

$p_2 = p_h - 8\,(\cos(-15°),\ \sin(-15°)) = (0.483 - 7.727,\ 0.129 + 2.071) = (-7.244,\ 2.200)$ m

So the trailer axle sits about 7.2 m behind and 2.2 m to the left of the rear axle. The viewer uses exactly these formulas to draw the trailer, and the planner uses them to check collisions for both bodies.

6Configuration Space

The configuration is the smallest set of numbers that fixes where every point of the robot is. For our rig:

$$q = (x_1,\ y_1,\ \theta_0,\ \theta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1 \times S^1$$

$\mathbb{R}^2$ is the plane (position), and each $S^1$ is a circle, because an angle of 360° is the same as 0°. The configuration space is four-dimensional, which matches the 4 degrees of freedom counted in Section 4.

COMMON MISTAKE

Do not say "the configuration has 8 numbers because there are two bodies and four wheels". Wheel rotation angles do not matter for where the truck is, and the hitch removes two numbers. Four is the answer.

7Rolling Without Slipping: the Nonholonomic Constraint

Rubber on asphalt grips: a wheel moves forwards or backwards along the direction it points, never sideways. In vector form, the wheel's velocity $\dot p = (\dot x, \dot y)$ has no component along the sideways direction $\hat n(\theta)$:

$$\dot p \cdot \hat n(\theta) = -\dot x\sin\theta + \dot y\cos\theta = 0$$

which is usually written

$$\dot x\,\sin\theta - \dot y\,\cos\theta = 0$$

Where the Constraints Act

  • Tractor rear axle (heading $\theta_0$): one constraint. This is why the rear axle moves exactly along the tractor's heading, which makes it the natural reference point.
  • Trailer axle (heading $\theta_1$): one constraint. This is what makes the trailer follow the hitch.
  • Front (steered) axle: its constraint is what links the steering angle to the turning rate, $\dot\theta_0 = v\tan\delta / L_1$. Person B derives it; for you, it is the reason $\delta$ appears as an input.

Written together, the constraints have the Pfaffian form $A(q)\,\dot q = 0$: they restrict the velocities $\dot q$, and the restriction depends on where you are, $q$.

Holonomic or Nonholonomic?

Holonomic constraintNonholonomic constraint
RestrictsPositions: some configurations are impossibleVelocities: some directions of motion are impossible right now
Example hereThe hitch: the two bodies must meet at one pointThe wheels: no sideways velocity

Why the Wheel Constraint Cannot Be Integrated

If the no-slip rule could be integrated into a position equation, some positions would be forbidden. They are not: a car can end up one parking space to the side of where it started, with the same heading, without its wheels ever sliding. That is parallel parking:

8Two Inputs, Four States: Still Controllable

Count again: 4 configuration coordinates, minus 2 no-slip constraints (rear axle and trailer axle) leaves 2 directions in which the rig can move at any instant. Those are exactly our two inputs: drive along the heading ($v$) and change the heading by steering ($\delta$).

Two inputs, four states: is it still possible to reach every pose? Yes. Switching between the two motions produces motion in new directions, the way "forward-left, reverse-right" in Figure 3 produced sideways motion. Mathematically, combining the two input vector fields (their Lie bracket) generates the missing directions, and Chow's theorem says that if repeated brackets span all directions, every configuration is reachable. For the car and for the tractor-trailer they do.

SAY IT LIKE THIS

"Four coordinates, two no-slip constraints, so only two free motions at any instant, which are speed and steering. Even so, by combining them the truck can reach any position and heading. That is why parking is possible at all: you cannot slide a truck sideways into a bay, but you can always manoeuvre into it."

COMMON MISTAKE

Do not call the system "not controllable" because it has fewer inputs than states. Fewer inputs means it cannot move in every direction instantly; it does not mean it cannot reach every configuration.

9Choosing the State and the Inputs

The State Vector

$$X = \begin{pmatrix}x_1\\ y_1\\ \theta_0\\ \psi\end{pmatrix}$$
SymbolMeaningWhy this choice
$x_1,\ y_1$Centre of the tractor's rear axle, in the world frame (m)Its velocity is purely along the heading (no-slip), so its equations are the simplest
$\theta_0$Tractor heading, from the $x$ axis, anticlockwise positiveTogether with $x_1, y_1$ it fixes the tractor
$\psi = \theta_0 - \theta_1$Hitch angle: tractor heading minus trailer headingIt is the safety quantity: a jackknife is $|\psi|$ too large, so we limit $|\psi| \le 60°$

Why $\psi$ and not $\theta_1$? They carry the same information ($\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \psi$), so either gives a valid state. We pick $\psi$ because the constraint we care about is on $\psi$, a real truck measures it with a hitch-angle sensor, and the instability (Part 2) is an equation in $\psi$ alone.

Sign convention: $\psi > 0$ means the tractor is turned anticlockwise (to the left) relative to the trailer. $\psi = 0$ means the rig is straight.

The Input Vector

$$U = \begin{pmatrix} v\\ \delta\end{pmatrix}$$
SymbolMeaningLimit and why
$v$Signed speed of the rear axle: positive forward, negative in reverse (m/s)$|v| \le 2$ m/s: yard speed, where the no-slip assumption holds
$\delta$Front-wheel steering angle relative to the tractor, positive to the left$|\delta| \le 35°$: the steering lock of the truck

These are the two things a driver controls. Our guidance converts $\delta$ into steering-wheel turns (a 20 : 1 steering ratio, so full lock is about 1.9 turns) and $v$ into km/h.

$L_1$Tractor wheelbase (rear axle to front axle)4.0 m

Why is the hitch ahead of the rear axle? On a real semi-truck the fifth wheel sits slightly ahead of the drive axle so the trailer's weight presses on the driven wheels. It adds a small term to the equations (Part 2).

10How to Draw the Model by Hand

Practise this until you can do it in about a minute on a whiteboard. Draw in this order, so every angle has a clear reference line. Exaggerate the angles (15° to 30°) so the arcs are easy to see.

1

Tractor. Draw a rectangle tilted up about 15°. Put a dot near its back for the rear-axle centre and label it $(x_1, y_1)$. Draw the tractor's centre line dashed, and extend it past the front. Ahead of the tractor, draw a short horizontal line and mark the angle up to the centre line: that is $\theta_0$.

2

Hitch and trailer. Mark the hitch as a small circle just ahead of the rear axle (a short distance $d$). From the hitch, draw the trailer as a longer rectangle going backwards, tilted the other way (about −15°). Put a dot at the trailer axle near its back end. From that dot draw a horizontal line and mark the angle to the trailer's centre line: that is $\theta_1$.

3

Hitch angle and steering. Extend the trailer's centre line forward through the hitch (dotted). The angle at the hitch between this extension and the tractor's centre line is $\psi$. At the front axle, draw a wheel turned relative to the tractor; the angle between the tractor's axis and the wheel is $\delta$.

4

Lengths and speed. Draw a dimension arrow from the rear axle to the front axle ($L_1$) and from the hitch to the trailer axle ($L_2$). Draw an arrow along the tractor for $v$ (say "negative when reversing").

COMMON MISTAKES WHEN DRAWING
  • Measuring $\psi$ from the trailer's centre line behind the hitch. $\psi$ is between the tractor's axis and the trailer's axis extended forward through the hitch.
  • Putting the hitch at the tractor's back bumper. It is just ahead of the rear axle ($d$ = 0.5 m).
  • Measuring $\delta$ from the world $x$ axis. $\delta$ is relative to the tractor.
  • Measuring $L_2$ from the trailer's front edge. $L_2$ runs from the hitch to the trailer axle.
  • Drawing $\theta_0$ and $\psi$ arcs on top of each other at the rear axle. Draw $\theta_0$ ahead of the tractor (the heading is the same anywhere along the body).
QUICK CHECK AFTER DRAWING

Point at your drawing and say: "$\theta_0$ is $15°$, $\theta_1$ is $-15°$, so $\psi = \theta_0 - \theta_1 = 30°$." If the three angles you drew are consistent with this sum, the drawing is right.

12Questions the Professor May Ask

Why is the system called nonholonomic?
Because its constraints restrict velocities, not positions, and cannot be integrated into position equations. No sideways velocity at each axle limits how it moves, but every pose remains reachable.
How many degrees of freedom does the rig have?
Four configuration coordinates: 3 + 3 for two planar bodies minus 2 for the revolute joint. At any instant only 2 of them can change freely, because there are 2 no-slip constraints; those 2 are the inputs.
Why did you choose the rear axle as the reference point?
The no-slip constraint makes its velocity point exactly along the heading, so $\dot x_1 = v\cos\theta_0$ and $\dot y_1 = v\sin\theta_0$, the simplest possible equations. Any other point would add terms in $\dot\theta_0$.
Why $\psi$ instead of the trailer heading $\theta_1$?
Same information, since $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \psi$. But the safety limit is on $\psi$, the instability is an equation in $\psi$, and a real truck measures $\psi$ directly.
Is the system controllable with only two inputs?
Yes. The Lie brackets of the two input vector fields span all four directions, so by Chow's theorem every configuration is reachable. Parallel parking is the everyday example.
Why not a dynamic model?
At yard speeds (≤ 2 m/s) tyre slip is a few centimetres, well inside our 0.25 m clearance margin. The kinematic model is accurate there and needs no tyre or mass data.
What is the difference between the hitch constraint and the wheel constraint?
The hitch constraint is holonomic: the two bodies must share one point, which removes two coordinates. The wheel constraints are nonholonomic: they only forbid sideways velocity.
What is the configuration space?
$\mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1 \times S^1$: a position in the plane and two angles, each living on a circle.
What happens with two trailers?
Three bodies, two joints: 9 − 4 = 5 coordinates; three axles give three no-slip constraints, leaving 2 free motions. Still the same two inputs, and still controllable, but with one more unstable hitch.

13Check Yourself

  1. If $\theta_0 = 40°$ and $\theta_1 = 25°$, what is $\psi$? Is the tractor turned left or right relative to the trailer?
  2. The rear axle is at (10, 5) m, $\theta_0 = 90°$ and $\psi = -20°$. Find $\theta_1$, the hitch position and the trailer-axle position.
  3. Count the configuration coordinates and the instantaneous free motions of a car (one body, front-wheel steering).
  4. Write the no-slip constraint for the trailer axle, whose position is $(x_2, y_2)$.
  5. True or false: a nonholonomic robot cannot reach some positions.
  6. Why does a revolute joint remove two freedoms, not one?

Answers

  1. $\psi = 40° - 25° = 15°$. Positive, so the tractor is turned to the left (anticlockwise) relative to the trailer.
  2. $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \psi = 90° + 20° = 110°$. Hitch: $(10 + 0.5\cos 90°,\ 5 + 0.5\sin 90°) = (10,\ 5.5)$ m. Trailer axle: $(10 - 8\cos 110°,\ 5.5 - 8\sin 110°) = (12.74,\ -2.02)$ m.
  3. 3 coordinates ($x$, $y$, $\theta$), 1 no-slip constraint on the rear axle, so 2 free motions: drive and steer.
  4. $\dot x_2\sin\theta_1 - \dot y_2\cos\theta_1 = 0$.
  5. False. It can reach every position; it just cannot move in every direction at every instant.
  6. A pin forces two points to coincide, which is two equations ($x$ and $y$ equal). Rotation about the pin stays free.

14One-Page Cheat Sheet

The Robot

Two rigid bodies + one passive revolute joint. DOF: $3 + 3 - 2 = 4$. Configuration $q = (x_1, y_1, \theta_0, \theta_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1 \times S^1$.

Transforms

$\hat u(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $\hat n(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$. Hitch $p_h = p_1 + d\,\hat u(\theta_0)$. Trailer axle $p_2 = p_h - L_2\,\hat u(\theta_1)$, $\theta_1 = \theta_0 - \psi$.

No-Slip (Nonholonomic)

$\dot x\sin\theta - \dot y\cos\theta = 0$ at the rear axle and at the trailer axle. Velocity constraint, not integrable; pose still reachable (parallel parking, Chow's theorem).

State and Inputs

$X = [x_1, y_1, \theta_0, \psi]^T$, $U = [v, \delta]^T$. $v < 0$ in reverse. $\psi > 0$: tractor turned left of the trailer.

Numbers

$L_1$ = 4 m, $L_2$ = 8 m, $d$ = 0.5 m, $|\delta| \le 35°$, $|\psi| \le 60°$ (jackknife at 75°), $|v| \le 2$ m/s.

Three Sentences to Remember

(1) It is a wheeled robot: two bodies, one passive joint. (2) Wheels cannot slide, so it is nonholonomic but still reaches every pose. (3) Four states, two inputs; $\psi$ is a state because it is what we must keep below 60°.
